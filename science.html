The Science

The numbers a sunflower counts in.

0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13… Each number is the sum of the two before it. Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci, put the sequence before European readers in 1202. Nature had been using it for rather longer.

The golden ratio

Divide each number by the one before it.

The answers close in on a single value, φ (phi), about 1.618. It is the only proportion where the whole is to the larger part as the larger part is to the smaller. Cut a square from a rectangle of that shape and the piece left over has the same shape again, for ever. Join quarter-circles through those squares and you draw a golden spiral, which widens by φ every quarter turn.

This site is laid out on the same ratio: its column widths, its spacing steps and its type sizes all grow by φ.

The golden angle

Why 137.5 degrees?

A growing plant places each new seed, leaf or scale a fixed turn after the last. Divide a full circle by φ and take the smaller part: 137.508°. Seeds placed at that angle never settle into straight spokes, so they pack the head evenly. Count the spirals you see in a sunflower, clockwise and anticlockwise, and you will usually find neighbouring Fibonacci numbers, such as 34 and 55. Pine cones commonly show 8 and 13.

Two hands

Spirals turn both ways at once.

A sunflower head holds two families of spirals, one winding clockwise and one anticlockwise, the same seeds read two ways. Our grille is cut to the same idea: two spiral families of opposite hand, interlaced into one pattern.

A note on shells. The nautilus is often called a golden spiral. It is a logarithmic spiral, the same family, but measured shells grow by a different ratio than φ. We would rather be accurate than romantic, and the truth is still remarkable: the logarithmic spiral is how a living thing can grow without changing shape.

From seed head to speaker

What the spiral does to sound.

Inside a rectangular box, sound bounces between parallel walls and builds up at a handful of resonant frequencies. A spiral has no parallel walls. In our acoustic simulations the spiral chamber's first internal resonance sits lower than that of a box of the same volume, and its resonances are spread more evenly instead of piling up.

Outside, the spiral opens into a horn-like mouth that shapes where the sound goes. That is why the outer casing can widen or narrow the sound field, and why we think directional sound can help with one of live music's hardest problems: noise that reaches the neighbours before it reaches the audience.

This is biomimicry: learning from forms that have been tested by growth and time, then measuring whether they help.
